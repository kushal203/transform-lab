02 / THE IDEA

Why Use Transforms for Signals and Images?

Transforms give us a new view: they help us understand a changing signal and shrink an image while keeping its main details.

A / UNDERSTAND A CHANGING SIGNAL

From a mixed wave to “what happens when?”

04 / MATHEMATICAL VIEWS

What Does Each Transform Do?

A transform tries a set of simple patterns against the data. Its coefficients tell us which patterns match strongly.

01 / DFT · GLOBAL FREQUENCIES
X[k]=Σnx[n]e−j2πkn/N = ⟨x,φk⟩

The complex sinusoid basis φk[n]=ej2πkn/N tests one frequency. The coefficient is a dot product (correlation): large |X[k]| means strong match. Inverse DFT sums all basis waves back together.

Why the bins separate: Σn=0N−1ej2π(k−ℓ)n/N = N if k=ℓ, otherwise 0 (finite geometric series). This sum is zero for two different frequency bins. That is why each DFT coefficient measures its own frequency.
02 / STFT · WINDOW, THEN FOURIER
X[m,k]=Σnx[n]w[n−mH]e−j2πkn/L
S[m,k]=|X[m,k]|²

Cut the signal into overlapping pieces. Fade each piece at its ends with a Hann window, then calculate its DFT. One piece makes one column in the spectrogram. Short pieces show event timing; long pieces separate nearby pitches.

The trade-off: a short time window spreads energy across more frequencies; a long window spreads events across more time. The slider changes the real window used here.
03 / CWT · DILATE AND SLIDE
W(a,b)=1/√a ∫x(t)ψ*((t−b)/a)dt
ψ(u)=(1−u²)e−u²/2   (Mexican hat)

Stretch ψ by scale a, shift it to location b, correlate against the signal. A small scale looks for quick, sharp changes. A large scale looks for broader, slower changes. The scalogram shows how strong each match is at each time and scale.

Why divide by √a: stretching changes wave size, so this factor keeps its total energy the same.
04 / IMAGE · BLOCK DCT + HAAR DWT
DCT: C[u,v]=αuαv Σx,yf[x,y]cos…cos…
Haar: a=(x₀+x₁)/√2, d=(x₀−x₁)/√2

DCT projects each 8×8 block onto 2-D cosine patterns. Haar DWT applies average/detail filters across rows then columns, recursively splitting coarse averages into scales. Threshold small coefficients; inverse-transform to reconstruct.

Energy preservation: DCT and normalized Haar bases are orthonormal, so ‖x‖²=‖c‖² (Parseval). Thresholding discards selected coefficient energy; inverse transform places the remaining error back into pixels. This demo reports MSE / PSNR and retained coefficients.
05 / READING THE OUTPUT

How to Read the Results

Use the axes, peaks and image scores to tell what each calculation is showing.

A / SIGNAL RESULTS

Time and frequency are the clues

  • Waveform: x = time, y = amplitude. Taller swings mean a stronger signal.
  • DFT bars: x = frequency in Hz, bar height = strength. A tall peak marks a strong frequency.
  • STFT spectrogram: x = time, y = frequency. A sloping bright ridge is the chirp; a flat line is the steady tone; a bright vertical patch is the short burst.
  • CWT scalogram: x = time, y = scale (large scales at top). Bright areas show a strong wavelet match; larger scales show slower, broader changes.
  • Colour: dark means weak; brighter cyan/yellow means stronger. STFT window: shorter gives clearer timing; longer separates nearby frequencies.
B / IMAGE RESULTS

Fewer coefficients usually mean less detail

  • Coefficient map: each square is a transform value, not a pixel. Brighter = larger coefficient; thresholding removes small ones.
  • Reconstruction: the remaining coefficients are transformed back into an image. More removed values usually means more lost detail.
  • MSE: average pixel error; lower is closer to the original.
  • PSNR: quality score; higher is usually better. Retained count tells how many coefficients remain.
Fair comparison: freeze one signal frame; use the same source image for DCT and DWT.
